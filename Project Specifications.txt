\section{Executive Summary}
% ==================================================

\projectname{} is a multi-agent artificial intelligence system designed to
analyze and critically evaluate scientific claims made in research papers.

The system accepts a target research paper as input. Rather than simply
summarizing the paper, it identifies important and testable claims made by
the authors and investigates whether those claims are supported by the
broader scientific literature.

For every selected claim, the system searches academic and web sources for
related papers. These papers may support, contradict, qualify, replicate,
or otherwise investigate the original claim. The retrieved documents,
together with the original paper, are processed by an agentic
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) component that performs document
understanding, claim-aware retrieval, evidence extraction, and evidence
organization.

The resulting evidence is then provided to two adversarial agents. A
Support Agent constructs the strongest evidence-grounded argument in favor
of the claim, while an Attack Agent independently investigates how the
claim could be wrong, overstated, conditional, or methodologically weak.

A Critic Agent evaluates both sides. It checks whether the cited sources
actually support the arguments, whether the reasoning is valid, whether
different studies are being compared fairly, and whether the final
conclusion is stronger than the available evidence.

A Supervisor Agent manages the overall investigation. It maintains the
state of every claim and determines whether sufficient evidence exists or
whether another search, retrieval, or analysis cycle is necessary.

The final result is therefore not merely a summary. It is an
evidence-grounded scientific assessment containing the original claim,
supporting evidence, contradictory evidence, arguments from both sides,
critic analysis, confidence, and a final verdict.

\section{Introduction}
% ==================================================

Scientific papers communicate research findings through a combination of
experimental results, methodological choices, interpretations, and claims.
A paper may report that a proposed method improves performance, reduces
computational cost, generalizes to multiple datasets, or provides an
explanation for an observed phenomenon.

However, reading one paper is not always sufficient to determine how
strong these claims actually are.

A claim may appear convincing inside the original paper while the broader
literature contains:

\begin{itemize}
    \item independent studies that support the result,
    \item studies that obtain weaker results,
    \item failed replications,
    \item studies that identify limitations,
    \item alternative explanations,
    \item methodological criticisms,
    \item results that contradict the original finding,
    \item evidence that limits the claim to specific experimental conditions.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, scientific understanding requires more than document
summarization. It requires comparing evidence across multiple sources and
evaluating the reasoning connecting evidence to conclusions.

The proposed system addresses this problem through a multi-agent
architecture in which different agents have explicitly different
objectives.

The system is designed around the following principle:

\begin{quote}
\textit{A scientific claim should not only be summarized; it should be
investigated, challenged, and evaluated against independent evidence.}
\end{quote}

% ==================================================
\section{Problem Statement}
% ==================================================

The central problem addressed by this project is the difficulty of
automatically determining whether scientific claims in a research paper
are sufficiently supported by the broader literature.

Traditional paper summarization systems generally answer questions such
as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item What problem does the paper address?
    \item What method does it propose?
    \item What experiments were performed?
    \item What were the reported results?
\end{itemize}

These capabilities are useful but do not directly answer:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Is the claim supported by independent research?
    \item Are there papers that contradict the claim?
    \item Under what conditions does the claim hold?
    \item Are the reported improvements statistically or experimentally
    meaningful?
    \item Are supporting papers actually comparable?
    \item Are the conclusions stronger than the evidence?
    \item Are there alternative explanations for the observed results?
\end{itemize}

The project therefore focuses on \textbf{scientific claim verification and
adversarial evidence analysis} rather than ordinary paper summarization.

% ==================================================
\section{Project Motivation}
% ==================================================

Research literature grows rapidly, making it difficult for a researcher
to manually investigate every claim across the relevant literature.

A researcher interested in one particular claim may need to perform the
following process manually:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Read the target paper.
    \item Identify its important claims.
    \item Search for related papers.
    \item Read those papers.
    \item Determine whether they support or contradict the claim.
    \item Compare experimental settings.
    \item Find methodological weaknesses.
    \item Investigate alternative explanations.
    \item Verify that citations actually support the arguments.
    \item Decide how strong the overall evidence is.
\end{enumerate}

This process is time-consuming and requires significant expertise.

The proposed system aims to automate a substantial part of this
investigation while preserving explicit evidence and source traceability.

The goal is not to replace scientific judgment. Instead, the system acts
as an automated research assistant that constructs an organized evidence
base and exposes both supporting and opposing interpretations of a claim.

% ==================================================
\section{Project Objectives}
% ==================================================

The primary objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Automatically identify important scientific claims in a target
    research paper.

    \item Convert vague textual statements into structured and testable
    propositions.

    \item Search academic and web sources for relevant external literature.

    \item Retrieve evidence from both the original paper and externally
    discovered papers.

    \item Use claim-aware and agentic retrieval rather than relying only
    on fixed-size text chunks.

    \item Construct independent supporting and opposing arguments.

    \item Detect contradictions and limitations in the literature.

    \item Verify the relationship between arguments and their cited
    evidence.

    \item Detect overgeneralization and invalid comparisons.

    \item Iteratively search for additional evidence when the available
    evidence is insufficient.

    \item Produce a transparent final assessment containing sources and
    evidence.

    \item Compare the proposed multi-agent architecture against a
    single-agent baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Overview}
% ==================================================

The complete system consists of seven specialized agents:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Supervisor Agent
    \item Claim Analyst
    \item Paper Search Agent
    \item RAG / Evidence Agent
    \item Support Agent
    \item Attack Agent
    \item Critic Agent
\end{enumerate}

The overall workflow is:

\begin{verbatim}
Target Paper
    |
    v
Supervisor
    |
    v
Claim Analyst
    |
    v
Paper Search Agent
    |
    +-------------------------+
    |                         |
    v                         v
Target Paper              Web Papers
    |                         |
    +------------+------------+
                 |
                 v
        RAG / Evidence Agent
                 |
                 v
          Evidence Store
                 |
          +------+------+
          |             |
          v             v
      Support         Attack
       Agent           Agent
          |             |
          +------+------+
                 |
                 v
              Critic
                 |
          +------+------+
          |             |
          v             v
       Verdict       Need More
                     Evidence
                         |
                         v
                     Supervisor
\end{verbatim}

The architecture is intentionally not a simple sequential pipeline. The
system can return to earlier stages when the Critic determines that the
available evidence is insufficient.

% ==================================================
\section{Why a Multi-Agent Architecture?}
% ==================================================

The project does not use multiple agents merely to increase the number of
LLM calls. Each agent has a different objective and a different
responsibility.

The central division is:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.2cm}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{9.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Agent} & \textbf{Core Question} \\
\midrule
Supervisor & What should happen next? \\
Claim Analyst & What exactly are we testing? \\
Paper Search Agent & What relevant research exists? \\
RAG / Evidence Agent & What evidence inside these papers is relevant? \\
Support Agent & Why might the claim be true? \\
Attack Agent & Why might the claim be wrong or overstated? \\
Critic Agent & Which evidence and arguments actually hold up? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Responsibilities of the proposed agents}
\end{table}

The most important multi-agent interaction occurs between the Support,
Attack, and Critic agents.

The Support Agent is explicitly optimized toward constructing the
strongest defensible case for a claim.

The Attack Agent is explicitly optimized toward finding evidence and
reasoning that could invalidate, weaken, or qualify the claim.

The Critic then evaluates both sides.

This creates an adversarial reasoning loop:

\[
\text{Claim}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Support}\\
\text{Attack}
\end{cases}
\rightarrow
\text{Critic}
\rightarrow
\text{More Investigation or Verdict}
\]

This decomposition can be experimentally compared against a single-agent
system.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 1: Supervisor Agent}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The Supervisor Agent manages the investigation and maintains the global
state of the system.

It is not intended to perform all scientific reasoning itself.

Its main question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{What should the system do next?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Responsibilities}

The Supervisor maintains the state of each claim, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item number of supporting sources,
    \item number of contradictory sources,
    \item number of independent studies,
    \item evidence quality,
    \item unresolved questions,
    \item arguments generated,
    \item criticism results,
    \item confidence,
    \item current investigation status.
\end{itemize}

An example internal state is:

\begin{lstlisting}
Claim C3
Evidence found: 8
Supporting evidence: 5
Contradicting evidence: 3
Independent studies: 2
Evidence quality: Medium
Arguments evaluated: Yes
Confidence: 0.62
Status: Needs more evidence
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Decision Making}

The Supervisor may decide to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item perform another literature search,
    \item retrieve more evidence from an existing paper,
    \item request an independent replication search,
    \item ask the Attack Agent to investigate a contradiction,
    \item ask the Support Agent to strengthen an argument,
    \item send the current investigation to the Critic,
    \item finalize a claim.
\end{itemize}

The Supervisor therefore creates an adaptive workflow rather than a
strictly predetermined sequence.

\subsection{Why the Supervisor is Needed}

Without a Supervisor, the system could become a fixed pipeline where every
claim always passes through exactly the same sequence.

Scientific investigation is not always predictable. Some claims may have
abundant evidence, while others may require additional searches.

The Supervisor allows the system to respond to the current state of the
investigation.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 2: Claim Analyst}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The Claim Analyst determines what the target paper is actually claiming.

A research paper contains many statements, but not every statement should
be investigated.

The Claim Analyst extracts claims that are meaningful, testable, and
relevant to the paper's contribution.

\subsection{Example}

Suppose a paper states:

\begin{quote}
``Our proposed architecture achieves state-of-the-art performance on
three benchmark datasets.''
\end{quote}

The Claim Analyst transforms this into a structured proposition:

\begin{lstlisting}
Claim ID: C7

Subject:
Proposed architecture

Claim:
Achieves state-of-the-art performance

Benchmarks:
Dataset A
Dataset B
Dataset C

Metric:
Accuracy

Comparison:
Previous state-of-the-art methods

Conditions:
Authors' experimental setup
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Claim Types}

The agent can classify claims into categories such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item performance claims,
    \item efficiency claims,
    \item causal claims,
    \item generalization claims,
    \item methodological claims,
    \item novelty claims.
\end{itemize}

For example:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Performance: ``Method X improves accuracy.''
    \item Efficiency: ``Method X reduces computational cost.''
    \item Causal: ``Component X causes the observed improvement.''
    \item Generalization: ``Method X works across different domains.''
\end{itemize}

This structured representation is essential because evidence retrieval
depends on the exact proposition being investigated.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 3: Paper Search Agent}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The Paper Search Agent is responsible for discovering external literature
related to the claims extracted from the target paper.

Its core question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{What other research exists about this claim?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Search Strategy}

The agent does not rely on one search query.

For a claim such as:

\begin{quote}
``Method X improves reasoning performance.''
\end{quote}

it may generate multiple search objectives:

\begin{lstlisting}
"Method X" reasoning performance
"Method X" benchmark results
"Method X" limitations
"Method X" replication
"Method X" failure
"Method X" comparison
"Method X" alternative
\end{lstlisting}

The search agent can use appropriate academic and web search sources.

\subsection{Adversarial Search}

A major feature of the search agent is that it should search from both
sides of a claim.

For supporting evidence:

\begin{lstlisting}
"X improves"
"X outperforms"
"X effectiveness"
"X replication"
\end{lstlisting}

For contradictory evidence:

\begin{lstlisting}
"X limitations"
"X fails"
"X no improvement"
"X critique"
"X benchmark problems"
\end{lstlisting}

This is important because searching only for confirming evidence can
create confirmation bias.

\subsection{Output}

The Paper Search Agent returns candidate papers, metadata, and relevance
information.

It does not perform deep evidence analysis. Its responsibility is
literature discovery.

The RAG / Evidence Agent is responsible for determining what information
inside those papers is actually useful.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 4: RAG / Evidence Agent}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The RAG / Evidence Agent processes both the original target paper and the
externally discovered papers.

Its core question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{What evidence inside these documents is relevant to the claim?}
\end{quote}

This component combines Retrieval-Augmented Generation with agentic
document processing.

\subsection{Responsibilities}

The RAG / Evidence Agent performs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item document parsing,
    \item document structural analysis,
    \item candidate evidence identification,
    \item agentic or claim-aware chunking,
    \item embeddings,
    \item retrieval,
    \item reranking,
    \item evidence extraction,
    \item evidence bundling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Processing Both Target and External Papers}

The system should use the same evidence-processing pipeline for:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item the target paper,
    \item papers discovered by the Paper Search Agent.
\end{enumerate}

Conceptually:

\begin{verbatim}
Documents
    |
    +----------+----------+
    |                     |
Target Paper          External Papers
    |                     |
    +----------+----------+
               |
               v
          RAG Agent
               |
       +-------+-------+
       |       |       |
      Parse  Chunk  Structure
       |       |       |
       +-------+-------+
               |
               v
     Claim-aware Retrieval
               |
               v
            Reranking
               |
               v
       Evidence Extraction
               |
               v
         Evidence Bundles
\end{verbatim}

% ==================================================
\subsection{Agentic Chunking}
% ==================================================

Traditional RAG systems often split documents using fixed token sizes.

For example:

\begin{verbatim}
500 tokens -> Chunk 1
500 tokens -> Chunk 2
500 tokens -> Chunk 3
...
\end{verbatim}

This can be problematic for scientific papers because important evidence
may span multiple sections.

For example, a scientific claim may depend on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a method description,
    \item an experimental setup,
    \item a result table,
    \item a paragraph interpreting the result,
    \item a limitation in the discussion.
\end{itemize}

The proposed system therefore uses claim-aware or agentic evidence
retrieval.

Given a claim such as:

\begin{quote}
``Method X improves accuracy over Method Y.''
\end{quote}

the agent can determine that the investigation requires:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the description of Method X,
    \item the baseline Method Y,
    \item the experimental setup,
    \item the dataset,
    \item the reported accuracy,
    \item statistical information where available,
    \item independent replications.
\end{itemize}

Instead of returning arbitrary chunks, the system constructs an
\textbf{evidence bundle}.

\subsection{Evidence Bundle}

An evidence bundle may have the following structure:

\begin{lstlisting}
Evidence ID: E17

Claim:
Method X improves accuracy.

Source:
Paper B

Evidence:
Table 3 reports X = 84.2% and Y = 79.1%.

Context:
Dataset = CIFAR-100
Training budget = 200 epochs
Metric = Accuracy

Location:
Page 7, Table 3

Relationship:
SUPPORTS
\end{lstlisting}

The system can combine multiple related sections into one evidence unit.

Thus, the important concept is not merely dynamic chunking, but
\textbf{claim-aware evidence assembly}.

% ==================================================
\section{Evidence Store}
% ==================================================

All extracted evidence is stored in a structured evidence repository.

The evidence store acts as the shared state between the research agents.

A conceptual representation is:

\begin{lstlisting}
Claim
|
+-- Evidence
|     |
|     +-- Source
|     +-- Location
|     +-- Evidence type
|     +-- Relationship
|     +-- Experimental context
|
+-- Supporting papers
|
+-- Contradicting papers
|
+-- Replications
|
+-- Arguments
\end{lstlisting}

An evidence record may contain:

\begin{itemize}
    \item evidence ID,
    \item claim ID,
    \item source paper,
    \item page or section,
    \item text or structured result,
    \item evidence type,
    \item relationship to claim,
    \item experimental conditions,
    \item confidence,
    \item provenance information.
\end{itemize}

The evidence store is important because agents should communicate through
structured evidence rather than repeatedly passing entire documents or
unstructured conversations.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 5: Support Agent}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The Support Agent constructs the strongest defensible case that a claim is
true.

Its central question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Why might this claim be true?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Responsibilities}

The agent considers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item strong supporting studies,
    \item independent replications,
    \item consistency between studies,
    \item methodological support,
    \item conditions under which the claim holds.
\end{itemize}

Example:

\begin{lstlisting}
Claim C1

Argument FOR:

1. Paper A independently reproduced the improvement.
2. Paper B observed the same trend on another dataset.
3. An ablation study suggests that component X
   contributes significantly to the improvement.

Strength:
Strong

Limitation:
Evidence is concentrated around benchmark family Y.
\end{lstlisting}

The Support Agent is not expected to simply summarize positive evidence.
It must construct a logically connected argument where important
statements are grounded in evidence.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 6: Attack Agent}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The Attack Agent investigates how the claim could be wrong, overstated,
conditional, or methodologically weak.

Its central question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Why might this claim be wrong or weaker than it appears?}
\end{quote}

It is therefore more than a ``devil's advocate.''

\subsection{Attack Dimensions}

The agent can investigate:

\begin{itemize}
    \item contradictory experimental results,
    \item failed replications,
    \item methodological weaknesses,
    \item dataset bias,
    \item benchmark contamination,
    \item alternative explanations,
    \item statistical weaknesses,
    \item overgeneralization,
    \item hidden experimental conditions,
    \item weak or misleading citations.
\end{itemize}

For example, suppose the original paper claims:

\begin{quote}
``Method X is better than Method Y.''
\end{quote}

The Attack Agent may discover:

\begin{quote}
``The reported improvement only occurs when Method X receives
substantially more training computation.''
\end{quote}

The claim may therefore need to be qualified.

\subsection{Independent Investigation}

The Attack Agent should be capable of requesting or performing additional
searches when the existing evidence does not provide sufficient
counter-evidence.

This is important because the Attack Agent should not merely criticize
the evidence selected by the Support Agent.

It should be able to investigate the literature independently.

% ==================================================
\section{Agent 7: Critic Agent}
% ==================================================

\subsection{Purpose}

The Critic Agent acts as the scientific judge of the investigation.

Its central question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Which evidence and arguments actually hold up?}
\end{quote}

It receives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the original claim,
    \item evidence from the target paper,
    \item external evidence,
    \item the Support Agent's argument,
    \item the Attack Agent's argument,
    \item source and provenance information.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evidence Validation}

The Critic checks whether a source actually supports what an agent says.

For example:

\begin{quote}
Support Agent: ``Paper A proves Method X is superior.''
\end{quote}

The Critic may discover that Paper A used a different dataset.

It can therefore classify the argument as only partially supported.

\subsection{Citation Validation}

The Critic checks whether:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the cited paper is relevant,
    \item the cited passage contains the claimed evidence,
    \item tables and figures are interpreted correctly,
    \item the source is being used in the correct context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reasoning Validation}

The Critic checks for logical overreach.

For example:

\begin{lstlisting}
Evidence:
X performs better than Y on Dataset A.

Argument:
X is universally better than Y.

Critic:
Invalid generalization.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Comparison Validation}

Different studies may appear contradictory while actually using different:

\begin{itemize}
    \item datasets,
    \item training budgets,
    \item model sizes,
    \item evaluation metrics,
    \item experimental conditions.
\end{itemize}

The Critic therefore evaluates whether comparisons between studies are
legitimate.

% ==================================================
\section{Final Verdict}
% ==================================================

The Critic can produce a final assessment such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Supported
    \item Partially Supported
    \item Unsupported
    \item Inconclusive
\end{itemize}

The verdict should contain:

\begin{lstlisting}
Claim:
Method X improves reasoning performance.

Verdict:
Partially Supported

Confidence:
0.74

Supporting Evidence:
Paper A
Paper B
Paper C

Contradicting Evidence:
Paper D
Paper E

Key Issue:
Improvement is consistent on benchmark X,
but evidence on other benchmarks is mixed.

Strongest Supporting Argument:
...

Strongest Counterargument:
...

Critic Assessment:
The original claim appears stronger than
what the broader literature supports.

Final Assessment:
Supported under specific experimental conditions.
General superiority is not established.
\end{lstlisting}

Every important evidence statement should retain a link to its source.

% ==================================================
\section{Iterative Investigation Loop}
% ==================================================

The system is designed to support iterative investigation rather than
always following one fixed path.

The core loop is:

\[
\text{Claim}
\rightarrow
\text{Search}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence Retrieval}
\rightarrow
\text{Support/Attack}
\rightarrow
\text{Critic}
\]

The Critic may determine that the evidence is insufficient.

In that case:

\[
\text{Critic}
\rightarrow
\text{Supervisor}
\rightarrow
\text{Additional Search}
\rightarrow
\text{Additional Retrieval}
\rightarrow
\text{Critic}
\]

For example:

\begin{lstlisting}
Claim C3

Supporting studies: 5
Contradicting studies: 1
Independent replications: 0

Critic:
Evidence insufficient to establish generality.

Supervisor:
Request independent replication search.

Paper Search Agent:
Search for independent replication.

RAG Agent:
Extract relevant evidence.

Attack Agent:
Evaluate newly discovered evidence.

Critic:
Re-evaluate claim.
\end{lstlisting}

This feedback mechanism is one of the main characteristics that makes the
system agentic.

\section{System Functionality}
% ==================================================

The complete user-facing workflow can be divided into the following
stages.

\subsection{Stage 1: Paper Upload}

The user provides a research paper.

Possible input formats include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item PDF,
    \item supported digital academic documents,
    \item optionally a paper repository URL or related source.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 2: Paper Understanding}

The system parses the document and identifies its structure, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item abstract,
    \item introduction,
    \item related work,
    \item methodology,
    \item experiments,
    \item results,
    \item discussion,
    \item limitations,
    \item appendix.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 3: Claim Extraction}

The Claim Analyst identifies important scientific claims.

Each claim receives a unique identifier.

\subsection{Stage 4: Literature Discovery}

For each claim, the Paper Search Agent generates appropriate search
queries and searches for relevant literature.

\subsection{Stage 5: Evidence Retrieval}

The RAG / Evidence Agent processes the target paper and external papers.

It retrieves the sections, paragraphs, tables, figures, and experimental
information needed to evaluate the claim.

\subsection{Stage 6: Evidence Organization}

Evidence is stored with provenance and relationships such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item SUPPORTS,
    \item CONTRADICTS,
    \item QUALIFIES,
    \item REPLICATES,
    \item PROVIDES\_ALTERNATIVE\_EXPLANATION.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 7: Adversarial Analysis}

The Support Agent constructs the strongest case for the claim.

The Attack Agent constructs the strongest case against it.

\subsection{Stage 8: Critical Evaluation}

The Critic validates the evidence, citations, comparisons, and reasoning.

\subsection{Stage 9: Additional Investigation}

If evidence is insufficient, the Supervisor triggers another investigation
cycle.

\subsection{Stage 10: Final Report}

The system produces a structured scientific assessment.

% ==================================================
\section{Example End-to-End Scenario}
% ==================================================

Consider a hypothetical paper claiming:

\begin{quote}
``Method X significantly improves reasoning performance compared with
previous approaches.''
\end{quote}

The system processes the claim as follows.

\subsection{Claim Analyst}

It formalizes the claim:

\begin{lstlisting}
Subject:
Method X

Claim:
Method X improves reasoning performance.

Comparison:
Previous approaches

Evaluation:
Reasoning benchmarks
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Paper Search Agent}

It searches for:

\begin{lstlisting}
Method X reasoning benchmark
Method X comparison
Method X replication
Method X limitations
Method X failure
Method X no improvement
\end{lstlisting}

It discovers several papers.

\subsection{RAG Agent}

The agent processes those papers and extracts evidence such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item reported improvements,
    \item experimental conditions,
    \item benchmark results,
    \item failed replications,
    \item limitations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Support Agent}

It identifies three independent studies supporting the improvement.

\subsection{Attack Agent}

It identifies two studies where the improvement is substantially smaller
and another study showing no significant improvement under different
conditions.

\subsection{Critic Agent}

The Critic determines that the supporting studies primarily use one
benchmark family, while contradictory studies use different evaluation
conditions.

The final verdict may therefore be:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Partially Supported.} The evidence supports an improvement under
specific benchmark and experimental conditions, but the available
literature does not establish that Method X provides a general improvement
across all reasoning settings.
\end{quote}

This demonstrates the intended behavior of the system.

\section{Agent Communication and Shared State}
% ==================================================

The agents should not communicate only through free-form natural language.
Important information should be represented in structured state.

A simplified claim object may contain:

\begin{lstlisting}
{
  "claim_id": "C3",
  "claim_text": "...",
  "claim_type": "performance",
  "status": "under_investigation",
  "supporting_evidence": [],
  "contradicting_evidence": [],
  "support_arguments": [],
  "attack_arguments": [],
  "critic_findings": [],
  "confidence": 0.0
}
\end{lstlisting}

This allows the Supervisor to understand the current investigation state
without relying on the entire conversation history.

The evidence store acts as a shared memory layer.

% ==================================================
\section{RAG Architecture}
% ==================================================

The RAG component is not simply a vector database.

Its conceptual architecture is:

\begin{verbatim}
Claim
    |
    v
Retrieval Objective
    |
    v
Candidate Documents
    |
    v
Structural Analysis
    |
    v
Candidate Evidence Units
    |
    v
Claim-aware Retrieval
    |
    v
Reranking
    |
    v
Evidence Extraction
    |
    v
Evidence Bundles
\end{verbatim}

Traditional RAG generally follows:

\[
\text{Query}
\rightarrow
\text{Retrieve Chunks}
\rightarrow
\text{LLM}
\]

The proposed system aims for:

\[
\text{Claim}
\rightarrow
\text{Determine Required Evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{Retrieve}
\rightarrow
\text{Combine Context}
\rightarrow
\text{Validate}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence Bundle}
\]

This makes retrieval more closely aligned with the scientific verification
task.

% ==================================================
\section{Web Search and Academic Search}
% ==================================================

The Paper Search Agent is responsible for discovering external research.

The architecture intentionally separates:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{document discovery}, and
    \item \textbf{evidence retrieval}.
\end{enumerate}

The Paper Search Agent answers:

\begin{quote}
``Which papers should we investigate?''
\end{quote}

The RAG / Evidence Agent answers:

\begin{quote}
``What evidence inside these papers is relevant to the claim?''
\end{quote}

This separation avoids creating one overly broad agent responsible for
searching, reading, reasoning, and judging simultaneously.

\section{Evidence Relationships}
% ==================================================

Each piece of evidence should be classified relative to the investigated
claim.

Possible relationships include:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4cm}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Relationship} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
SUPPORTS & Evidence provides direct or indirect support for the claim. \\
CONTRADICTS & Evidence conflicts with the claim under comparable conditions. \\
QUALIFIES & Evidence suggests the claim is true only under specific conditions. \\
REPLICATES & Independent research obtains a similar result. \\
CHALLENGES & Evidence raises methodological or conceptual concerns. \\
ALTERNATIVE & Evidence provides another explanation for the observed result. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Possible evidence relationships}
\end{table}

This allows the final report to represent scientific uncertainty more
accurately than a simple true/false classification.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
% ==================================================

A major requirement of the project is to demonstrate that the multi-agent
architecture provides value beyond simply using one strong LLM.

Therefore, the system should be evaluated against appropriate baselines.

\subsection{Baseline 1: Single-Agent System}

A single strong agent receives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the target paper,
    \item access to search,
    \item retrieved evidence.
\end{itemize}

It is asked to perform the complete claim verification task.

The proposed multi-agent system is then compared against this baseline.

\subsection{Baseline 2: Standard RAG}

A conventional RAG system using fixed-size or semantic chunks can be
compared with the claim-aware RAG component.

\subsection{Proposed System}

The full system contains:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Claim Analyst,
    \item Paper Search Agent,
    \item RAG / Evidence Agent,
    \item Support Agent,
    \item Attack Agent,
    \item Critic Agent,
    \item Supervisor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
% ==================================================

The project can be evaluated using several measurable metrics.

\subsection{Claim Verification Accuracy}

Measure whether the system's final verdict agrees with expert judgments
or a carefully constructed evaluation set.

\subsection{Evidence Retrieval Quality}

Measure whether retrieved evidence is actually relevant to the claim.

Possible metrics include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item precision,
    \item recall,
    \item relevance rate,
    \item evidence sufficiency.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Citation Correctness}

Measure whether claims made by the system are actually supported by the
cited source.

\subsection{Contradiction Detection}

Measure how effectively the system identifies literature that genuinely
contradicts or weakens the target claim.

\subsection{Argument Quality}

Evaluate the quality of Support and Attack arguments.

\subsection{Overgeneralization Detection}

Measure the system's ability to identify when a paper's conclusion is
stronger than the evidence justifies.

\subsection{Human Intervention}

Measure how often a human must intervene before a useful verdict is
produced.

\subsection{Cost}

Measure the total inference cost per investigated paper or claim.

This is especially important because multi-agent systems can require
multiple model calls.

\subsection{Latency}

Measure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item time to process a paper,
    \item time per claim,
    \item search time,
    \item retrieval time,
    \item final report generation time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions}
% ==================================================

The project can be evaluated around the following research questions.

\subsection{RQ1}

\textbf{Can a multi-agent system verify scientific claims more reliably
than a single-agent system with equivalent access to literature and
retrieval tools?}

\subsection{RQ2}

\textbf{Does claim-aware agentic evidence retrieval improve evidence
quality compared with fixed-size or conventional semantic chunking?}

\subsection{RQ3}

\textbf{Does adversarial reasoning between independent Support and Attack
agents improve detection of unsupported or overstated scientific claims?}

\subsection{RQ4}

\textbf{Does a Critic Agent reduce citation errors, invalid reasoning, and
overgeneralization in automatically generated scientific assessments?}

\subsection{RQ5}

\textbf{Can the system perform this investigation within practical
consumer-grade compute and inference-cost constraints?}

\section{Why the Project is Not Simply ``RAG + Agents''}
% ==================================================

A central concern with multi-agent projects is that agents may be added
without providing a meaningful architectural advantage.

This project attempts to avoid that problem through explicit functional
separation.

A simple system might perform:

\[
\text{Paper}
\rightarrow
\text{LLM}
\rightarrow
\text{Summary}
\]

A conventional RAG system might perform:

\[
\text{Paper}
\rightarrow
\text{Chunks}
\rightarrow
\text{Retriever}
\rightarrow
\text{LLM}
\]

The proposed system instead performs:

\[
\text{Paper}
\rightarrow
\text{Claims}
\rightarrow
\text{Independent Literature Discovery}
\rightarrow
\text{Claim-Aware Evidence Retrieval}
\rightarrow
\text{Adversarial Analysis}
\rightarrow
\text{Critical Validation}
\rightarrow
\text{Adaptive Investigation}
\rightarrow
\text{Verdict}
\]

The agents have competing or complementary objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Search Agent maximizes relevant literature discovery.
    \item Support Agent maximizes the strongest defensible supporting case.
    \item Attack Agent maximizes discovery of weaknesses and contradictions.
    \item Critic Agent minimizes unsupported reasoning.
    \item Supervisor manages uncertainty and determines when additional
    investigation is necessary.
\end{itemize}

This provides a measurable basis for evaluating whether the decomposition
is useful.

\section{Expected Challenges}
% ==================================================

The project contains several significant technical challenges.

\subsection{Claim Extraction}

Scientific claims can be ambiguous or spread across multiple sections.

\subsection{Evidence Retrieval}

The relevant evidence may not be located in the same paragraph as the
claim.

\subsection{Cross-Paper Comparison}

Two papers may report apparently conflicting results under substantially
different conditions.

\subsection{Citation Reliability}

An LLM may produce an argument that sounds reasonable but is not actually
supported by its citation.

\subsection{Search Quality}

Poor search queries can lead to incomplete or biased literature.

\subsection{Confirmation Bias}

If the system only searches for evidence that agrees with the original
paper, the resulting verdict becomes unreliable.

The independent Attack Agent is intended to address this issue.

\subsection{Inference Cost}

Seven agents do not imply seven times the quality. Excessive agent
interaction can increase latency and cost.

Therefore, the system should minimize unnecessary agent calls.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Evaluating scientific reasoning is more difficult than evaluating simple
question answering.

Expert-labeled claims and evidence relationships may therefore be needed
for a reliable benchmark.

% ==================================================
\section{Compute and Practicality Considerations}
% ==================================================

The system is intended to operate on consumer-grade hardware and
practical API budgets.

The architecture should avoid unnecessary use of expensive models.

Potential strategies include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item using smaller models for document preprocessing,
    \item using embeddings for inexpensive retrieval,
    \item using stronger models only for difficult reasoning stages,
    \item caching processed papers,
    \item avoiding repeated processing of the same document,
    \item limiting additional search cycles,
    \item storing structured evidence instead of repeatedly passing full
    papers to agents.
\end{itemize}

The system should measure both cost and latency.

The multi-agent architecture should only be considered successful if the
quality improvements justify its additional computational cost.

% ==================================================
\section{Failure Handling}
% ==================================================

The system should explicitly handle failure cases.

Examples include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item no relevant papers found,
    \item papers inaccessible,
    \item PDF parsing failure,
    \item contradictory experimental settings,
    \item insufficient evidence,
    \item unreliable citation,
    \item search results dominated by the original paper,
    \item duplicate papers,
    \item conflicting metadata,
    \item agent disagreement.
\end{itemize}

Rather than forcing a binary answer, the system should be capable of
returning:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Inconclusive:} insufficient reliable evidence was found to support
or reject the claim.
\end{quote}

This is preferable to producing a confident but unsupported verdict.

\section{Expected Contribution}
% ==================================================

The project is primarily a systems contribution rather than a proposal
for a new fundamental machine learning algorithm.

The expected contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A practical multi-agent architecture for scientific claim
    verification.

    \item A claim-aware evidence retrieval pipeline operating over both
    the original paper and dynamically discovered literature.

    \item An adversarial Support--Attack--Critic reasoning structure.

    \item A state-driven Supervisor capable of triggering additional
    investigation when evidence is insufficient.

    \item A structured evidence representation preserving provenance.

    \item An empirical comparison between single-agent and multi-agent
    claim verification.

    \item An empirical comparison between conventional chunking and
    claim-aware evidence retrieval.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project Scope}
% ==================================================

To keep the project feasible, the initial scope should focus on scientific
literature where reliable digital documents and metadata are available.

The initial system can focus on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item machine learning,
    \item artificial intelligence,
    \item computer science,
    \item related technical research.
\end{itemize}

The system should not initially attempt to verify every possible type of
scientific claim across every discipline.

Highly mathematical claims, specialized laboratory experiments, and
claims requiring physical experimentation may require additional domain
specific capabilities.

\section{Conclusion}
% ==================================================

The proposed Multi-Agent Scientific Claim Verification System addresses a
problem that is different from ordinary paper summarization.

Instead of asking only what a paper says, the system asks whether the
paper's important claims are supported by the broader literature and under
what conditions those claims remain valid.

The system decomposes this task into specialized responsibilities:

\begin{verbatim}
Supervisor
    |
Claim Analyst
    |
Paper Search Agent
    |
RAG / Evidence Agent
    |
Evidence Store
    |
+-----------+
|           |
Support    Attack
|           |
+-----------+
    |
Critic
    |
Verdict
\end{verbatim}

The key architectural feature is the adversarial interaction between
independent supporting and opposing analyses, followed by a Critic that
validates evidence and reasoning.

The RAG component extends beyond conventional fixed-size chunking by
performing claim-aware evidence retrieval and assembling evidence from
both the target paper and externally discovered literature.

The Supervisor makes the system adaptive by recognizing when additional
investigation is necessary.

Most importantly, the project can be evaluated experimentally rather than
only demonstrated visually. A strong single-agent baseline, conventional
RAG baseline, and ablation studies can determine whether each architectural
component provides measurable value.

The ultimate objective is therefore not to build a system that simply
produces persuasive scientific text, but a system that can construct,
challenge, verify, and transparently assess scientific claims using
evidence from the research literature.
